# A prize nobody entered

[Who gets to decide](README.md) · [City directory](../directory.md)

**Paper & Pine Books.** Paper & Pine awards a local writing prize to a manuscript submitted by a family member without the author's consent.

## Sadie Cross

AH-R1111 · Age 37 · Local writer and cleaner · Home — the streets around Foundry Lofts

**Life in the city.** “I still remember paying for a notebook meant for nobody else.” People around Lighthouse Cove know Sadie by a small Sunday habit. Sadie takes a few minutes by the low rocks, where conversation sounds different as the tide turns.

**In their own words.** “I wrote the piece to understand my brother's death. A prize makes that private purpose feel surprisingly expensive.”

**What Sadie wants.** To choose personally whether the manuscript leaves the family.

**Family.** [Amelia Kahn](../06/01-the-upstairs-lease.md#ah-r0759) is Sadie’s second cousin. Sadie chooses whether to use it. Amelia saves a place. The invitation means something because declining it has never ended the friendship between their households. Asked about *The upstairs lease*, Amelia says, “I wrote a budget in which nobody grows, gets ill or buys new shoes.”

**People in this story.** [Lane Bose](#ah-r1112) (relative who submitted the book). The person who entered the work has to put the decision back in the author's hands.

**Personality.** Sadie reads little signals of discomfort very accurately and has an unfortunate habit of overruling people for their own good. The friends who say stop are the ones Sadie trusts most.

**Appearance.** Sadie is easy to recognize by a long plait tucked inside the collar and a wide brow and a narrow jaw. Usually dressed in a washed violet jersey with a watch on a cracked leather band, Sadie draws breath as if to speak and lets someone else go first when listening.

**Under pressure**

- The prize organizer asks the writer to appear onstage. Sadie asks the organizer to wait for the writer's own answer.
- A neighbor offers to pay for a favor that was meant kindly. Sadie accepts a small agreed amount if paying lets the neighbor keep their pride.
- If told to back off, Sadie does so immediately and stays available at a distance the other person chooses.

## Lane Bose

AH-R1112 · Age 43 · Family caregiver · Home — the western blocks near Ferry Street Rooms

**Life in the city.** “I still remember posting a letter just before the afternoon pickup.” On Friday, near the downtown fountain, Lane keeps a little time free to hear who is playing at the far end of the park.

**In their own words.** “I posted it because the author never took chances. I'm learning the difference between a chance and a choice.”

**What Lane wants.** To return control of the submission to the writer.

**Family.** [Grey Garza](../03/03-the-widow-s-dividend.md#ah-r0327) is Lane’s stepsibling. Lane gets to change the plan. Grey offers the first invitation. They learned that a promise to visit is easier to keep when changing a time does not count as letting down the whole family. Asked about *The widow's dividend*, Grey says, “I forecast a loss and delayed moving home. One unexpected payment could bring my wife back to the coast.”

**People in this story.** [Sadie Cross](#ah-r1111) (author). The person who entered the work has to put the decision back in the author's hands.

**Personality.** Lane has a quick wit and an equally quick sense of shame. A joke is often Lane's way of finding out whether a room is friendly enough for a serious question.

**Appearance.** Lane is easy to recognize by a loose dark braid and a broad nose and a deep dimple on one cheek. Usually dressed in a mustard canvas overshirt with a pocket notebook shaped by years of sitting on it, Lane presses the lips together to keep a smile in when listening.

**Under pressure**

- A relative wants the manuscript returned unopened. Lane returns the pages privately without treating keeping them closed as a verdict on the work.
- The last bus leaves while an elderly stranger is still crossing toward it. Lane offers to walk to the next staffed place together, at the stranger's pace, to ask about another ride.
- If the joke lands badly, Lane seeks out the person it hurt before telling anybody why it was supposed to be funny.

## Aidan Henry

AH-R1113 · Age 55 · Prize juror · Home — the neighborhood around Seabright Court

**Life in the city.** “I still remember recognizing a relative's bread knife in a stranger's book.” People around Studio Boulevard know Aidan by a small Sunday habit. Aidan waits by the window where the whole crossing can be seen without leaning into the road.

**In their own words.** “I sat on the jury and recognized our family's kitchen in the scene. I should have said something before the vote.”

**What Aidan wants.** To disclose the juror's family connection.

**Family.** [Bina Marsh](../01/05-the-aquarium.md#ah-r0045) is Aidan’s younger cousin. Aidan is free to hang up first. Bina makes the call. Some of their best calls last less than a minute. A change of tone is sometimes all the news either one needs. Asked about *The aquarium*, Bina says, “He kept telling me the filter was humming wrong.”

**People in this story.** [Scout Gallagher](#ah-r1114). The juror's family recognition puts the runner-up in an awkward but legitimate position.

**Personality.** Aidan listens for the familiar version of a story and then for the one detail that changes it. Friends bring problems here when they want to be heard twice.

**Appearance.** On an ordinary day, look for a red windbreaker and a cloth patch so neatly matched it only shows in strong light. Aidan has a softly rounded chin under sharp cheekbones, with short copper curls. Aidan starts to lift a hand, then waits to be invited to speak during a conversation.

**Under pressure**

- The prize organizer asks the writer to appear onstage. Aidan asks the organizer to wait for the writer's own answer.
- A customer is short at the till while a queue forms. Aidan asks the cashier quietly which item can be set aside without making the customer explain their finances to the line.
- When a second witness disagrees, Aidan keeps the accounts separate until the witnesses have had a chance to meet.

## Scout Gallagher

AH-R1114 · Age 32 · Novelist and school aide · Home — the western blocks near Ferry Street Rooms

**Life in the city.** “I still remember entering a competition nobody in my family took seriously.” People around the downtown fountain know Scout by a small Wednesday habit. Scout keeps a little time free to hear who is playing at the far end of the park.

**In their own words.** “I came second. I don't want to win by someone else's withdrawal and I don't want a dishonest result.”

**What Scout wants.** To secure an honest result for the runner-up.

**Family.** [Loretta Osei](../09/03-the-million-dollar-flat.md#ah-r1229) is Scout’s aunt by marriage. Scout calls about good news first. Loretta is allowed to ask about the bad news afterward. Neither wants every family conversation to begin with a bill, a diagnosis or an apology. Asked about *The million-dollar flat*, Loretta says, “I am her sister. My own flat is barely worth half and it's the one with space for us both.”

**People in this story.** [Aidan Henry](#ah-r1113). The juror's family recognition puts the runner-up in an awkward but legitimate position.

**Personality.** Scout talks very little about private fear and recognizes it quickly in strangers. Offering a choice of seats is one of Scout's ways of saying that it is all right to leave.

**Appearance.** Scout has a round face with a pronounced chin. Look for a soft fringe that falls across one eyebrow. On an ordinary day, the familiar outfit is a brown corduroy jacket with a comb in a sleeve sewn into the lining. In conversation Scout grins on one side while the eyes stay serious.

**Under pressure**

- A relative wants the manuscript returned unopened. Scout returns the pages privately without treating keeping them closed as a verdict on the work.
- A friend asks for a loan due back before payday. Scout agrees on a sum and a date with the friend in private, keeping the arrangement out of family gossip.
- When pressed to explain someone else's silence, Scout says only what happened and lets the absent person keep their reasons.

## Lou Diaz

AH-R1115 · Age 49 · Literary organizer · Home — the streets around Foundry Lofts

**Life in the city.** “I still remember agreeing a print deposit over the price of a coffee.” Friday often includes a stop at the blocks below Foundry Lofts, where Lou checks the little hand-lettered notices around Harbour Supply.

**In their own words.** “I promised the prize money to a printer before the author answered. I will pay the printer myself.”

**What Lou wants.** To settle the printer's bill without pressuring the author.

**Family.** [Nabil Reed](../01/09-the-porch-light.md#ah-r0087) is Lou’s cousin raised in the same household. Lou sets the time to leave. Nabil picks the meeting place. It lets them stay close without turning a family visit into the loss of an entire day. They have both had stretches when that distinction mattered. Asked about *The porch light*, Nabil says, “We rebuilt the post around that live lamp during a blackout anniversary.”

**People in this story.** [Dora Garza](#ah-r1119). The printer's costs can be settled without taking away the writer's closed room.

**Personality.** Lou prefers exact instructions at work and pleasant surprises at home. The two standards occasionally collide, especially when a relative thinks dropping by counts as notice.

**Appearance.** Lou has close-cropped dark curls, one cheek that creases before the other, and a favorite piece of clothing, a cream pullover. It usually goes with a little coil of soft measuring tape. When somebody else has the floor, Lou counts on the side of one finger where hardly anybody notices.

**Under pressure**

- The prize organizer asks the writer to appear onstage. Lou asks the organizer to wait for the writer's own answer.
- A witness is embarrassed about remembering only a little. Lou asks where they stood and what they could hear, leaving the rest open instead of filling it with guesses.
- When a visitor arrives unexpectedly, Lou clears one chair and says honestly how long the visit can last.

## Iva Hayes

AH-R1117 · Age 41 · Translator · Home — the streets inland from Pier Beach

**Life in the city.** “I still remember my first translation printed without my name.” People around the market by Market Flats know Iva by a small Saturday habit. Iva often waits until the delivery baskets are stacked before going in.

**In their own words.** “I translated two pages as a favor. My name is on the cover and the author's wishes aren't.”

**What Iva wants.** To obtain the author's approval for the translation.

**Family.** [Jon Solis](../01/10-the-thirteenth-doughnut.md#ah-r0095) is Iva’s second cousin. Iva can always ask for five minutes alone. Jon asks before bringing somebody along. It keeps a conversation between two people from unexpectedly turning into a family committee. Asked about *The thirteenth doughnut*, Jon says, “I kept the lemon one in a bag all day for my wife.”

**People in this story.** [Pat Garrett](#ah-r1120). An older diarist and a translator compare what genuinely consenting to publication feels like.

**Personality.** Iva often says yes too quickly out of pride in being useful. The people closest to Iva have learned to ask again the next morning, when the answer is more likely to account for sleep.

**Appearance.** Iva has an aquiline nose and a square chin. Look for short black hair swept back. On an ordinary day, the familiar outfit is a charcoal zip cardigan with a copper-colored clasp that has been repaired by hand. In conversation Iva touches a knuckle to the chin when searching for a word.

**Under pressure**

- The prize organizer asks the writer to appear onstage. Iva asks the organizer to wait for the writer's own answer.
- An unfamiliar person asks for directions but cannot read the street signs. Iva walks with them as far as the first landmark and points to the next one from there.
- When two people ask for the same hour, Iva rings both of them before quietly counting on somebody else to fill in.

## Leona Navarro

AH-R1118 · Age 38 · Neighborhood reading host · Home — Old Town

**Life in the city.** “I still remember sharing an unfinished story with its first unknowing subject.” Sunday often includes a stop at the corner outside Coastal Barber, where Leona usually checks the board before the regulars give a more colorful account of the news.

**In their own words.** “I would like the piece read once to the people in it before anybody publishes a line.”

**What Leona wants.** To arrange a reading limited to the people represented.

**Family.** [Dolores Yeo](../05/14-the-split-visit.md#ah-r0732) is Leona’s cousin. Leona asks the question the rest of the family avoided. Dolores chooses an ordinary story to tell. They tend to get further with an important subject when it arrives in the middle of talk about dinner or work. Asked about *The split visit*, Dolores says, “I am paid for only one visit. I used the extra pair of hands to hang Dad's old framed diploma.”

**People in this story.** [Elliot Cotton](#ah-r1116). The private reading gives someone portrayed in the book a place to raise concerns before publication.

**Personality.** Leona thinks best while walking. Sitting beneath someone's office clock makes Leona sound more certain than they feel, an effect close friends have learned to distrust.

**Appearance.** On an ordinary day, look for a soft blue checked shirt and a cloth patch so neatly matched it only shows in strong light. Leona has a wide brow and a narrow jaw, with a high puff tied with plain cord. Leona holds eye contact until the other person has finished during a conversation.

**Under pressure**

- A relative wants the manuscript returned unopened. Leona returns the pages privately without treating keeping them closed as a verdict on the work.
- A street musician can't finish a familiar song. Leona starts a quiet conversation with the most impatient onlookers so the musician can recover their own time.
- When family demands an immediate answer, Leona asks them to walk to the corner together and listen until the corner comes.

## Dora Garza

AH-R1119 · Age 51 · Bookshop proprietor · Home — the western residential streets

**Life in the city.** “I still remember locking the shop doors so a writer could change their mind.” On Monday, near the courtyard by Ferry Street Rooms, Dora prefers the bench where the sound of the shop door reaches before the visitor does.

**In their own words.** “I own the bookshop. I have given the author the keys after closing so the decision doesn't happen in front of a queue.”

**What Dora wants.** To provide a closed room for the decision.

**Family.** [Edward Pike](../06/10-one-paid-trial.md#ah-r0844) is Dora’s cousin raised in the same household. Dora knows which ones can be shared. Edward keeps the photographs. They enjoy remembering people in public and also know what it feels like to have a private afternoon turned into an anecdote for strangers. Asked about *One paid trial*, Edward says, “I tasted both loaves blind and chose my ex's.”

**People in this story.** [Lou Diaz](#ah-r1115). The printer's costs can be settled without taking away the writer's closed room.

**Personality.** Dora gets protective of anybody who comes into a meeting unprepared. Dora privately wishes people would notice the amount of preparation behind this apparent ease.

**Appearance.** Dora has two dimples that appear only with a full smile. Look for a center part and straight chestnut hair. On an ordinary day, the familiar outfit is a charcoal zip cardigan with a tiny red thread tied through the lowest buttonhole. In conversation Dora looks toward the door whenever a name is called.

**Under pressure**

- The prize organizer asks the writer to appear onstage. Dora asks the organizer to wait for the writer's own answer.
- A diner goes silent in the middle of a noisy family celebration. Dora sits beside them without expecting a performance and asks if they want to go outside.
- If a colleague lies to save face, Dora offers one private chance to correct it before the next conversation.

## Pat Garrett

AH-R1120 · Age 74 · Retired diarist · Home — the western residential streets

**Life in the city.** “I still remember writing a diary through six house moves.” On Wednesday, near the blocks below Foundry Lofts, Pat checks the little hand-lettered notices around Harbour Supply.

**In their own words.** “I kept a diary nobody read for sixty years. I would like to enter mine under my own steam.”

**What Pat wants.** To submit a diary by personal choice.

**Family.** [Graham Jackson](../03/05-rent-in-a-biscuit-tin.md#ah-r0343) is Pat’s cousin. Pat is free to hang up first. Graham makes the call. Some of their best calls last less than a minute. A change of tone is sometimes all the news either one needs. Asked about *Rent in a biscuit tin*, Graham says, “The handwriting on my envelope is my late wife's.”

**People in this story.** [Iva Hayes](#ah-r1117). An older diarist and a translator compare what genuinely consenting to publication feels like.

**Personality.** Pat uses precise names for tools and very approximate names for feelings. People who know Pat well listen to what gets repaired after a difficult conversation.

**Appearance.** Pat has salt-and-pepper curls, a crooked front tooth visible when laughing, and a favorite piece of clothing, a cream pullover. It usually goes with a replacement button a shade darker than the others. When somebody else has the floor, Pat rubs a thumb along the edge of the nearest pocket.

**Under pressure**

- A relative wants the manuscript returned unopened. Pat returns the pages privately without treating keeping them closed as a verdict on the work.
- An envelope of wages is found under a café chair. Pat brings the envelope to the counter unopened and stays long enough to make a note of who accepted it.
- After a family quarrel, Pat brings back the borrowed thing in good condition and tries the conversation again without an audience.
